Experimental effects of a restrictive housing step-down program

Can people move to less restrictive housing without increasing misconduct?

A randomized evaluation in the Oregon Department of Corrections compared the Step-Up Program (SUP) with continued placement in the Intensive Management Unit (IMU), examining misconduct both during restrictive housing and after return to the general prison population.

Research questions
Question 01

Does the Step-Up Program change misconduct while people are still in restrictive housing?

The study examines all, violent, and non-violent misconduct using both official records and self-reports.

Question 02

Does moving to Step-Up change misconduct after return to general population housing?

The study follows official misconduct for six months after participants leave restrictive housing.

FIGURE 1 · STUDY DESIGN

Everyone started in the same restrictive setting

People already housed in the Intensive Management Unit were randomly assigned to remain in the IMU or to be offered a transfer to the Step-Up Program. The study then measured misconduct during restrictive housing and, using official records, for six months after return to the general population.

THE INTERVENTION

SUP changed the conditions of restrictive housing

The Step-Up Program combined environmental changes, additional opportunities and programming, and a different approach to staff–prisoner interaction. It was intended to provide a less restrictive and more rehabilitative setting than the IMU.

5–6 hours
targeted daily out-of-cell time in SUP, compared with about 1 hour in IMU

More than a housing change

SUP combined changes to the physical environment, social interaction, programming, case management, recreation, and staff culture.

01 · OUT-OF-CELL TIME

More time outside the cell

The program was designed to provide 5–6 hours of out-of-cell time per day, compared with about 1 hour available in the IMU.

02 · SOCIAL CONTACT

More interaction

Participants had more opportunities for social interaction, including outdoor recreation with others and greater access to staff.

03 · PROGRAMMING

More rehabilitative services

SUP expanded access to treatment and recreational activities, including Free Your Mind in Segregation, DBT, GOGI, Living Well, HHAAP, and Charting a New Course.

04 · CASE MANAGEMENT

More individualized support

A correctional case counselor coordinated treatment plans and matched participants with programs based on their risks and needs.

05 · STAFF APPROACH

A more humanistic officer role

Correctional officers in SUP were encouraged to adopt a more humanistic approach, including participating in activities such as card or video games with prisoners. The authors note that this did not occur in the IMU.

The intervention was a package

SUP was not a single treatment component. The study evaluates a bundle of changes in living conditions, socialization, treatment access, recreation, case management, and staff culture.

HOW TO READ THE RESULT

The key contrast is inside restrictive housing versus after return

The experimental results are more nuanced than a single “worked” or “didn’t work” finding.

01 · During restrictive housing

A mixed pattern

The official-record estimates were mostly null. Violent misconduct was the exception in the LATE analysis: b = +0.097, p = .043. The authors interpret this partly through greater opportunity for interpersonal contact in SUP.

02 · After return to general population

No detected group differences

The official-record estimates did not show statistically significant differences between SUP and IMU across all three outcomes. The ITT coefficients were −0.021, +0.024, and −0.001.

WHAT TO KEEP IN MIND

The experimental test was strong; implementation was not complete

The authors identify several reasons to interpret the findings in context.

COVID-19

Program disruption

Enrollment was paused from May 2020 through March 2021, and the SUP operated in a modified format during part of the pandemic.

PARTIAL UPTAKE

Not everyone assigned to SUP transferred

Only 52 of the 109 people assigned to SUP agreed to move, which is why the study reports both ITT and LATE estimates.

STAFFING

The intended dosage was not always achieved

Staffing shortages meant the program did not consistently reach its intended 5–6 hours of out-of-cell time per day.

SAMPLE SIZE

Modest statistical power

The study included 211 randomized participants, and the interviewed sample was smaller, limiting the ability to detect subtle effects in self-reported outcomes.
